# AI Literacy Deck — Outline

## For board meetings, staff training, or public programming

*This is the slide-by-slide outline. The finished deck (PDF/slides) is produced from this. Plain language throughout; no jargon without unpacking it. About 13 slides, 20 to 30 minutes with discussion.*

---

**Slide 1 — Title.** AI in the Public Library: What It Is, Where It Already Lives, and How We Govern It. [Library Name]. Presenter, date.

**Slide 2 — Why we are talking about this.** AI is already in our software, our databases, and our patrons' questions. The choice is not whether to engage it. The choice is whether we govern it on purpose or let scattered practice become de facto policy.

**Slide 3 — What AI actually is.** Software that produces text, images, summaries, recommendations, or rankings by recognizing patterns learned from large amounts of data. It is a pattern machine, not a knowing one. It is confident whether or not it is right.

**Slide 4 — Open vs closed, in one breath.** Closed: keeps what you type private, does not train on it. Open: may learn from what you type. Most free consumer tools are open. The key question is always: what am I putting into this tool?

**Slide 5 — Where AI already lives in our library.** Embedded tools (spell-check, smart compose). Vendor-added AI in the catalog, discovery layer, and databases. Staff using consumer tools to draft and brainstorm. Patrons asking us how to use it. Three of these four are already happening before any policy exists.

**Slide 6 — The three things we protect.** Patron privacy (what people read and ask is confidential, by ethics and usually by law). Intellectual freedom (a tool that ranks or filters is making access decisions). Equity (AI literacy is a public good we can help spread or accidentally hoard).

**Slide 7 — The real risks, named plainly.** Patron data leaking into an open tool. A recommendation engine quietly narrowing what patrons see. AI-generated content published without review. Vendor AI switched on by default without anyone deciding. Efficiency gains quietly becoming the case for cutting staff. Energy and water costs nobody weighed.

**Slide 8 — What AI literacy means for patrons.** Helping the public tell a pattern machine from a knowing one, spot confident errors, protect their own privacy, and use these tools without being used by them. This is reference and instruction work. It is what we already do, applied to a new tool.

**Slide 9 — Our answer: AI stewardship.** Not a ban, not a free-for-all. A management function that assigns, supports, documents, reviews, and explains AI decisions before they scatter. Six responsibilities: readiness, tool registry, impact review, staff guidance, public literacy, transparent reporting.

**Slide 10 — The tool registry.** One list of the AI tools and features in use, who owns each, what data it touches, and on what terms. Not a static spreadsheet: a maintained record with an owner and a history. Libraries already know how to do this. It is authority work.

**Slide 11 — Minimum viable governance.** For a small library: one shared list, one named signer, and one rule. No AI tool reaches patrons or patron data without an entry. That is governance that scales down without falling apart.

**Slide 12 — What this asks of you.** Know what you put into a tool. Ask before the five things on the one-pager. Treat AI output as a first draft. Be transparent. Raise concerns; no one is penalized for it.

**Slide 13 — Discussion.** What AI tools are you already using? What worries you? What would help? Point to the policy, the one-pager, and the registry.
