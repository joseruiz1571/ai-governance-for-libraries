# AI Literacy & Stewardship Deck — Outline

## For board meetings, staff training, or public programming

*This is the slide-by-slide outline. The finished deck (PDF/slides) is produced from this. Plain language throughout; no jargon without unpacking it. About 15–20 minutes presenting time.*

**Slide 1 — Title.** AI in the Public Library: What It Is, Where It Already Lives, and How We Govern It. [Library Name]. Presenter, date.

**Slide 2 — Why we are talking about this.** AI is already in our software, our discovery catalog, our databases, and our patrons' questions. The choice is not whether to engage it. The choice is whether we govern AI-enabled uses intentionally—connecting evidence, local authority, and community knowledge—or let scattered practice and vendor updates become de facto policy.

**Slide 3 — What AI actually is.** Software that produces text, images, summaries, query interpretations, recommendations, or rankings by recognizing statistical patterns in large datasets. It is a pattern machine, not a knowing one. It sounds confident whether or not it is right.

**Slide 4 — Open vs. closed, and why vendor silence matters.** Closed: binding terms and technical controls keep inputs private and exclude model training. Open or unverified: inputs may be retained or used to train models. Most free consumer tools are open. In library governance, unresolved vendor terms or vendor nonresponse never expand permission.

**Slide 5 — Where AI already lives in our library.** Routine internal tools (spell-check, drafting). Vendor-added AI in the catalog, discovery layer, and databases. Staff using generative tools for workflows. Patrons asking us to help evaluate AI outputs. Approval of a product for one routine use does not authorize new embedded features that shape patron access.

**Slide 6 — The core library commitments we protect.** Patron privacy (what people read, search, and ask is confidential by ethics and state law). Intellectual freedom (a feature that interprets queries, ranks, or filters is making access decisions). Equity and accessibility (preserving human mediation and non-AI service routes across languages and needs).

**Slide 7 — The real risks, named plainly.** Patron search queries leaking into unverified vendor processors. Conversational discovery narrowing what patrons find or missing relevant holdings. Vendor updates adding personalized ranking without review. Efficiency claims used to displace staff rather than support service. Environmental and supply-chain labor costs nobody weighed.

**Slide 8 — AI literacy vs. community participation in governance.** Public AI literacy helps patrons spot confident errors, protect their privacy, and use tools critically—an extension of reference and instruction work. **Community participation in governance goes further:** attending a workshop does not itself confer influence over library choices. For consequential AI uses, community members and staff help define the service problem, weigh non-AI alternatives, and set the criteria for acceptable performance before a feature is activated.

**Slide 9 — Our answer: AI stewardship.** A continuing management function within existing governance that connects six responsibilities across the library: **Readiness assessment**, **Registry maintenance**, **Impact review** (Accuracy, Autonomy, Recourse, Enforceability), **Staff guidance**, **Public AI literacy and civic engagement**, and **Transparent reporting**.

**Slide 10 — The AI Tool & Use Registry.** An authority record ([Program for Cooperative Cataloging NACO Manual](https://www.loc.gov/aba/pcc/naco/documents/NACOParticipantsManual.pdf)) for AI-enabled uses—not a static spreadsheet. It separates **authorized disposition** (what is permitted now and under which conditions) from **review state** (what evaluation is underway), records how community and staff input shaped the criteria, links each unresolved question to the activity it prevents, and names the authority able to revise or stop the use.

**Slide 11 — Scaling to local capacity (staged feasibility).** Instead of an arbitrary 90-day calendar, libraries work through four completion-based stages within agreed workload limits: (1) *Establish scope and authority*, (2) *Develop evidence and participation*, (3) *Decide and communicate*, and (4) *Examine feasibility* (checking staff clarity and whether an uninvolved colleague can reconstruct the decision from its written record).

**Slide 12 — What this asks of staff.** Know the difference between grouped low-risk internal uses and uses that require an explicit disposition first. Remember that starting a review or running an isolated staff test does not authorize a public pilot. Treat AI output as a starting point, preserve non-AI service routes for patrons, and report errors or vendor changes so the decision can be reconsidered.

**Slide 13 — Discussion and participation.** Which service problems actually need AI, and where would better existing search or staff support work better? What criteria should an AI feature have to meet before it reaches patrons? Point to the policy, the one-pager, and the registry.
