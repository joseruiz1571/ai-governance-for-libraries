# AI Governance for Public Libraries

[![DOI](https://zenodo.org/badge/DOI/10.5281/zenodo.22330685.svg)](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22330685)

A practical, values-grounded AI stewardship and governance kit for public libraries, free to use and adapt under CC BY 4.0.

*Version 0.3.0 – aligned with the revised AI stewardship governance model (see How to cite below).*

## What This Is

Public libraries hold the trust of the people they serve: what patrons read, search, ask, and borrow is confidential by professional ethics and, in most states, by law. As AI tools become embedded in everyday software, in the catalog, and in subscription databases, libraries need clear, continuing responsibility for how those uses are evaluated, authorized, and reconsidered.

This kit gives a library's board, director, and staff what they need to adopt an AI use policy, evaluate AI-enabled uses, involve staff and community knowledge in defining priorities and success criteria, train staff, and maintain an accountable decision record of what is permitted and under which conditions. Nothing here requires specialized technical knowledge, and nothing costs money.

The kit is consistent with the [American Library Association's Guidance on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Libraries](https://www.ala.org/tools/standards-and-guidelines/guidance-use-artificial-intelligence-libraries) (adopted by the ALA Council, July 2026), including its sustainability, labor, intellectual freedom, and privacy commitments, as well as the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1) and the [GAO AI Accountability Framework](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-519sp). It remains an independent resource, not an ALA publication.

It is the companion toolkit to the article *"AI Stewardship in Public Libraries: A Proposed Governance Model"* by Witt Salley and Jose Ruiz-Vazquez.

## What Is In the Kit

**AI_Use_Policy_Template.md**
The full policy template, ready for the board to adapt and adopt. Eleven sections covering scope (around AI-enabled uses and material reliance), professional grounding, authorized and prohibited uses, proportionate community and staff participation, intellectual freedom, patron privacy, data stewardship, time-limited exceptions for imposed platforms, oversight, training, and public literacy. Replace the bracketed library name, adjust what does not fit, and it is ready for a vote.

**AI_Tool_Registry_Template.md**
The keystone. A working template for the decision record every library should keep: the AI-enabled uses across services and workflows, their separate **authorized disposition** and **review state**, assigned authority functions, how staff and community participation shaped the purpose and evaluation criteria, what evidence supports the decision, and what uncertainty prevents further activation. Built on the discipline libraries already know—authority control ([Program for Cooperative Cataloging NACO Manual](https://www.loc.gov/aba/pcc/naco/documents/NACOParticipantsManual.pdf))—so it does not decay into a static spreadsheet.

**AI_Tool_Evaluation_Checklist.md**
A structured checklist for evaluating any new AI-enabled use, or any vendor-added AI feature, before authorization. Its output becomes a registry record. Carries pre-selection community and staff participation checks, the four-question impact review (**Accuracy**, **Autonomy**, **Recourse**, and **Enforceability**), intellectual freedom and stewardship-cost checks, vendor response deadlines, and workload tracking.

**AI_Quick_Reference_OnePager.md**
A single printable page: what staff may use AI for freely, what requires an authorized decision first (including activating embedded features or relying on outputs that shape patron access or ranking), and what is never permitted. Post it at the desk.

**Sample_Board_Resolution.md**
Ready-to-read motion language for adopting the policy at a board meeting and establishing staged implementation within local capacity.

**AI_Literacy_Deck_Outline.md**
A 13-slide literacy and governance outline for board meetings, staff training, or public programming. Plain-language explanations of what AI is, where it already lives in the library, where the real risks are, and how community participation in governance differs from attending a workshop.

## How to Use It: A Staged First Implementation Cycle

Instead of an arbitrary calendar deadline, a first implementation cycle uses four stages driven by completion conditions so libraries can scale the work to their available capacity:

1. **Establish scope and authority.** Adapt the policy template to your governance structure and select a manageable initial scope (for example, one consequential use and one grouped entry for comparable low-risk internal tools). Identify the service owner, reviewer, approver, registry maintainer, records custodian, and authorized risk acceptor where applicable (one person may hold several functions). Set an agreed workload allocation and complete a baseline procedural clarity check. *Stage complete when responsibility, scope, and work limits are explicit.*
2. **Develop evidence and participation.** Invite proportionate community and staff input on the service problem, non-AI or existing alternatives, and what counts as acceptable performance before locking in success criteria. Run the evaluation checklist across Accuracy, Autonomy, Recourse, and Enforceability, and set a vendor-response deadline. Record supported conclusions separately from missing evidence, linking each material gap to the specific activity it prevents. *Stage complete when the reviewer can explain the available basis and limits for a decision.*
3. **Decide and communicate.** Record the **authorized disposition** separately from the **review state** in the registry (beginning a review grants no permission, and an internal staff exercise does not authorize a public pilot). Translate the approved conditions into staff guidance using the one-pager and provide an accessible public explanation with a clear correction and recourse route. *Stage complete when permission is explicit and responsible staff can act on it.*
4. **Examine feasibility.** Compare initial and final registry entries, actual staff effort against the agreed workload allocation, and pre/post staff procedural clarity. Ask an uninvolved colleague to reconstruct a decision from its written record alone, examine whether community and staff contributions received a reasoned response, and record a closing judgment to continue, revise, pause, or stop the added process. *Stage complete when a responsible actor records the feasibility decision and next reconsideration point.*

The minimum that makes this real is one maintained registry and one rule: no AI-enabled use reaches patrons, patron data, or consequential decisions without an authorized disposition.
